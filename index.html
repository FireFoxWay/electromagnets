The idea

What is an electromagnet?

A fridge magnet is stuck being a magnet forever. You can't turn it off.

An electromagnet is different. It's only a magnet while electricity is flowing through it. Switch the power off and the magnet disappears.

Here's the whole secret: when electricity flows through a wire, it makes magnetism. One straight wire makes a tiny bit. But if you coil the wire up into lots of loops, all those tiny bits stack up into a real magnet.

Slide an iron nail into the middle of the coil and it gets way stronger, because iron is magnetic and it joins in.

So you need three things: a coil of wire, a battery, and an iron nail.

Three things to try

  1. Add more loops. Drag "loops of wire" from 5 up to 50. It gets stronger every step — each loop adds more magnetism.
  2. Add more batteries. More batteries push more electricity, so the magnet gets stronger. (In real life the wire also gets hot!)
  3. Change what's inside. Swap the iron nail for aluminum, then for nothing. The magnet almost dies. Only iron helps — aluminum isn't magnetic.

One more thing: press Flip the battery. The N and S ends swap places! That happens because the electricity is now going around the coil the other way.

Real life

Where they're hiding

You use electromagnets every day without noticing.

Doorbell

Pressing the button switches on an electromagnet that yanks a little hammer into a chime. That's the ding-dong.

Speakers

A coil behind the speaker cone flips its magnet ends super fast. The cone pushes the air back and forth, and that's sound.

Motors

Fans, blenders, electric cars, toy trains. They spin because an electromagnet keeps flipping and chasing a pull it can't catch.

MRI machines

A huge electromagnet in a hospital lets doctors see inside your body without cutting anything open.

Maglev trains

Magnets in the track push the train up so it floats above the rail. No wheels touching means it can go over 300 mph.

Junkyard cranes

Big enough to lift a whole car — and then drop it exactly where the driver wants it.

At home

Make a real one

Takes about 15 minutes. Do it with an adult.

You need

  • A big iron or steel nail
  • About 3 feet of coated copper wire
  • One D battery
  • Tape and a pile of paperclips

Steps

  1. Scrape the plastic coating off about an inch at each end of the wire, so you can see bare copper.
  2. Wrap the wire around the nail, always in the same direction. Count your loops!
  3. Tape one bare end to + on the battery and the other to −.
  4. Touch the nail to the paperclips and lift. Count how many come up.
  5. Pull one wire off the battery. The paperclips fall — the magnet is gone!
  6. Now change one thing (like adding more loops) and test again.

⚠ Be careful

  • The wire and battery get hot. Unhook it between tries.
  • Use a regular D battery only. Never a wall plug.
  • Never connect the two battery ends with just wire and no coil.
  • Keep it away from phones, laptops, and TVs.
